Learning topics

1. The digital divide: what it is and why it matters

The digital divide is the gap between people who can use the internet and technology confidently, and people who cannot — because of cost, coverage, language, disability, or skills. Closing it means designing for everyone, from the start: slow connections, older devices, screen readers, and multiple languages.

Why this matters to you

Nearly half the world is still offline or under-connected. When services move online — health, school, government, work — people without reliable access get left further behind. Small choices (lightweight pages, offline support, plain language) are what make inclusion real.

2. Getting online for less

You do not need the newest phone or the fastest plan. Learn about low-cost data packages, community Wi-Fi points, public library terminals, and apps that work well on weak connections. Tip: enable "data saver" in your browser and use text-based or "lite" versions of popular sites.

3. Safety & privacy basics

Your first line of defense is habits: use a long, unique password for every account; turn on two-step verification; update your phone and apps; and treat unexpected links and messages with suspicion. If something asks for money or passwords urgently, it is almost always a scam.

Three quick wins
  • Turn on two-step verification for email and banking.
  • Use a password manager instead of reusing passwords.
  • Check your privacy settings in social apps once a year.

4. Accessibility features already in your phone

Most phones include free tools: larger text, high-contrast display, screen reader (TalkBack / VoiceOver), voice typing, and magnification. You can usually find them under Settings → Accessibility. These features are not "extras" — they are how technology becomes usable for people with low vision, limited mobility, or who prefer listening to reading.

5. Using public services online

Government portals handle benefits, IDs, health records, and taxes. Before you start, collect your documents, use an official domain, and never share your password or OTP with anyone who calls or messages you. When a portal is hard to use, ask a trusted person or use the help line — good services have human support too.

6. AI for everyone — and where to stay careful

AI tools can translate, summarize, caption videos, and read text aloud — real inclusion gains. But they can also be wrong, and they train on data that reflects existing biases. Rule of thumb: use AI for drafting and understanding, verify facts yourself, and never paste secrets into tools. AI should ask before acting, and you should always be able to say no.

A simple AI checklist
  • Assume the AI can be wrong; check important claims.
  • Do not share passwords, IDs, or bank details with chatbots.
  • Keep a human in the loop for decisions that affect your life.
